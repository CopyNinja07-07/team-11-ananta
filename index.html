The problem. Buildings pay for electricity and water that never do useful work. The familiar cases (taps, tanks, lights) are well studied. Some waste happens in places no one looks: inside a wall, inside a cable joint, or in the hour after supply comes back.

What we did. We re-read our review of 261 studies for what the authors left out. We extracted 831 limitation and future-work statements from 150 papers, grouped them into 19 themes, and checked by hand whether any paper detects, measures or prices each candidate waste. We then searched outside the review for the nearest prior work on each.

What we found.

  • Four kinds of waste are named or implied in the literature and measured by none of it inside a building.
  • All four trace back to one assumption. Detection methods assume electricity and water arrive without interruption. Where supply stops and starts every day, new kinds of waste appear and the standard methods cannot see them.
  • All four can be read from a small set of low-cost measurements (voltage and current at two points, the difference between phase and neutral current, tank level, pump power, and the water meter), provided the system first knows whether supply is on, off or has just returned.
  • Each gap comes with a test. Open datasets and simple bench rigs are enough to measure every one of them, and Section 10 sets out how.

1 Introduction

1.1 The problem in plain terms

A building pays one bill for electricity and one for water. Each bill is a single total. It does not say how much of the electricity reached an appliance, or how much of the water reached a tap. Research on waste detection has mostly asked which appliance or which tap is at fault. That question assumes all the waste happens at the point of use.

Some of it does not. Electricity can be lost on the way to the appliance. Water can be lost on the way to the tank. And when supply is cut and restored, as it is every day in much of India, the building behaves differently for a while in ways that cost energy and water. None of this shows up as a device left on, so detectors built to find devices left on do not find it.

1.2 How this fits the project

The project brief asks for a system that detects anomalies such as leaks and after-hours use, forecasts consumption, and explains savings in plain language. The four gaps in this paper extend the meaning of "anomaly" in that brief. They are waste the building pays for, that a facility manager could act on, and that current methods miss. Table 1 maps them to the brief.

Table 1. Each gap falls under a requirement the brief already contains.

1.3 What counts as hidden waste

We used three tests. A gap had to pass all of them.

  • It is not visible at the point of use. No appliance, tap or tank shows it.
  • It has a consequence beyond the bill. The waste causes something that is noticed, even though the waste itself is not.
  • No reviewed study measures it inside a building. Nearby work may exist on the grid side or in a laboratory, and we cite it where we found it.

1.4 Scope

This paper reviews published work and public data. Worked examples use illustrative numbers to show how each quantity is calculated. It covers non-residential buildings and gives particular attention to Jammu and Kashmir.

2 How the gaps were found

The starting point was an earlier review of 261 studies on building energy anomaly detection, water leak detection, forecasting, language-model explanations and low-cost sensing. We had full text for 88 of them and abstracts for the other 173.

  • Extraction. From each paper we took the sentences where authors state what they did not do, what they assumed, or what they leave for future work. This gave 831 sentences from 150 papers.
  • Grouping. We sorted the sentences into 19 themes by keyword, then read the contexts.
  • Hand check. For each candidate waste we separated papers that only name it from papers that detect, measure or price it.
  • Outside search. For each of the four gaps we searched the web for the closest existing work and read the sources we cite.

All counts in this paper refer to this review of 261 studies.

3 The shared root: supply that stops and starts

Almost every method in the review was developed where electricity and water are always available. The large open datasets come from such places. Three assumptions follow, and each one hides a kind of waste.

Table 3. Three assumptions made by standard methods, and what each one hides where supply is intermittent.
AssumptionWhat standard methods doWhat this hides
Power is always onTreat an outage as missing data and delete or fill it [1], [2]Everything the building does in the hour after power returns
Water is always in the pipeLook for leaks as flow at night when nobody uses waterLeaks that only run during a short supply window; air counted by the meter
Energy on the meter reaches a loadModel the building as a sum of appliancesEnergy lost in the wiring between the meter and the appliance

In Jammu and Kashmir, load-shedding of 8 to 10 hours a day is reported in our corpus (IN008), and municipal water in Indian cities commonly runs for 2 to 6 hours a day (IN037). Buildings there live in the conditions the methods leave out.

4 Electricity gap one: heat in the wiring

In simple words

Some electricity is lost as heat inside the building's own wires before it reaches any machine. The building pays for that electricity and gets nothing from it. The same heat is how electrical fires begin.

4.1 What actually happens

  1. Current leaves the meter and travels through cable, switchgear, joints and socket terminals before it reaches an appliance.
  2. Every part of that path has a small electrical resistance. Current flowing through resistance produces heat. In a sound circuit the amount is small.
  3. Over years, screws loosen, aluminium terminals oxidise, and circuits sized for fans and lights are loaded with air conditioners and heaters. The resistance at those points rises.
  4. More resistance means more heat at the same current. The heat is produced inside a switchboard or a wall, where no one sees it.
  5. The meter sits upstream of all this, so it counts the lost energy and the building pays for it.
  6. Heat ages the insulation around the joint, which raises resistance further. Left alone, the joint can reach the point where insulation fails and a short circuit starts a fire.

4.2 Why nobody notices

  • The bill shows one total, so wiring loss is indistinguishable from use.
  • Energy monitors put one meter on a circuit, which reads load and loss together.
  • Safety devices react to a fault. A joint that is only warm is not yet a fault.
  • People cannot see inside a switchboard or a wall.

4.3 What it causes

India's National Crime Records Bureau recorded 7,566 fire accidents in 2022, of which 1,567 (about 21%) were attributed to electrical short circuits. The same share was about 3% in 1996 [3]. Government office buildings are among the cases reported each year. Fire investigation looks at these events afterwards. Protection devices act at the moment of failure. The months of rising heat before that moment are not recorded by anything.

4.4 What the research does

  • In the review: transformer and cable losses are named in 12 studies, all at grid level. None measures loss in a building's own wiring. Low-cost metering studies place one meter per circuit, which cannot separate wiring loss from load.
  • Outside the review: thermal camera inspections find hot joints, but they are done once a year at best. Arc-fault devices trip when arcing begins. Recent laboratory work detects loose contacts at a smart meter's own terminals. We found no work that tracks the resistance of a building circuit over time and reports the loss as energy and cost.

4.5 How it can be measured

Two voltage readings and one current reading on the same circuit are enough.

Power lost in wiring = (voltage at board − voltage at load) x current
Resistance of the path = (voltage at board − voltage at load) / current   a rising trend means a joint is degrading

The second quantity is the useful one. In the illustrative case of Figure 1, 60 W over an eight-hour working day is about half a unit of electricity, which is a small cost for one circuit. The value lies in the trend. A path whose resistance has doubled since the last quarter is a maintenance job that can be scheduled before it becomes a fault. The cost figure matters when summed across every circuit in a building, and that sum has not been published for any Indian office we could find.

What an alert would say. "AC circuit, Room 12: 38 W is turning into heat in the wiring. The resistance of this circuit has doubled since March. Have the terminals checked." (illustrative)

5 Electricity gap two: the energy debt of a power cut

In simple words

A power cut does not simply switch consumption off. The building falls behind during the cut and catches up afterwards by using extra electricity. If the extra is nearly as large as what the cut saved, the cut saved almost nothing.

5.1 What actually happens

Load-shedding exists to save electricity when supply is short. The assumption is that a building cut off for two hours uses two hours' less energy. The sequence below shows why that is only partly true.

  1. The cut begins. Grid power stops. Inverters and UPS units take over essential loads and drain their batteries.
  2. The building drifts. With air conditioners or heaters off, rooms warm up in a Jammu summer or cool down in a Kashmir winter. Walls, furniture and air store that change.
  3. Power returns. Three things start together. Batteries recharge everything they supplied plus their conversion losses. Air conditioners or heaters run at full output to bring rooms back to their set temperature. Appliances whose switches were left on restart, including in rooms people have left.
  4. A peak forms. Because all of this begins in the same minutes, the building draws well above its usual load. Where the tariff has a demand charge, this peak can set it for the month.
  5. The record is cleaned. When the data is later analysed, the outage is treated as missing data and removed or filled in [1], [2]. The recovery hour is blended into normal operation.

5.2 The payback ratio

We propose describing each cut with one number.

Payback ratio = extra energy used after power returns / energy not used during the cut
Table 4. The payback ratio states whether a cut saved energy.
Payback ratioMeaning
Well below 1The cut saved most of the energy it was meant to save
Close to 1The cut saved little. It moved the consumption to a later hour, often into the evening peak
Above 1The building used more energy because of the cut than it would have without it

The ratio is not fixed by physics in either direction, which is why it has to be measured.

Table 5. Forces act on the payback ratio in both directions, and their net effect has not been measured.
Lowers the ratioRaises the ratio
A room that has drifted towards the outdoor temperature gains or loses less heat during the cutBatteries return less energy than is put into them
People who left during the cut do not come back, so some load never returnsAppliances restart in rooms that are now empty
A resistive heater has the same efficiency at any outputOccupants set a more extreme temperature to recover faster
Humidity that built up during the cut has to be removed as well as heat

An illustrative case: a building that normally draws 20 kW loses power for 2 hours, so 40 kWh is not used. Over the 3 hours after power returns it uses 36 kWh more than it normally would. The payback ratio is 0.9, and the cut saved 4 kWh, a tenth of what was assumed. Battery losses can push the ratio above 1, since a battery returns less energy than is put into it.

5.3 What the research does

  • Planned curtailment. Demand response research measures payback after air conditioners are deliberately curtailed, and has indices for it [4]. Those are voluntary, short, announced events on reliable grids.
  • The grid side. Power engineers study the surge when a feeder is re-energised, known as cold load pickup, to protect equipment. A South African simulation found that recharging household battery backups after load-shedding can raise demand enough to counteract the purpose of the cut [5]. Both look at the network, not at what one building pays.
  • In the review. Rebound appears only in the context of voluntary demand response (EW025, CI007, LX009). No study measures it after involuntary load-shedding, and the standard cleaning step removes the data needed to do so.

5.4 How it can be measured

The difficulty is the dashed line in Figure 2: what the building would have used with no cut. That is a forecasting problem, which the brief already includes. With a forecast trained on days without cuts, the grey and orange areas can be computed for every cut from the main meter alone, the start and end of each cut being read from the meter's voltage. Summed over a month and priced, the payback becomes a cost of unreliable supply for that building, split into battery recharge, thermal recovery and demand charge.

Jammu and Kashmir offers a comparison found in few other places. The same utility serves a cooling-dominated region and a heating-dominated one, so the thermal part of the debt can be studied in both directions.

What an alert would say. "Today's 2-hour cut saved 40 units. The building used 36 extra units in the 3 hours after power returned. Net saving: 4 units." (illustrative)

6 Water gap one: the pipe that is empty most of the day

In simple words

Where water comes for only an hour or two, the supply pipe is empty for the rest of the day. Leaks then run only during that hour and are hidden inside the tank filling. When water returns, the meter also counts the air that is pushed out of the pipe, and the building is billed for it.

6.1 What actually happens

Leak detection was developed for networks where pipes are always full and under pressure. In a building that receives water for an hour or two a day, the service pipe passes through three states every day, and each has its own problem.

  1. While supply is on, the pipe is under pressure and any crack loses water. This is the only time the leak runs, and it is also when the tank is filling, so the leak is mixed into a large legitimate flow.
  2. While supply is off, the pipe drains and fills with air. There is no pressure and no flow. A method that looks for flow at night finds none and reports no leak. Meanwhile, low or negative pressure allows water from the surrounding soil to enter through the same cracks.
  3. When supply returns, the water pushes the air out through the building's connection. A mechanical meter turns as the air passes and adds it to the bill. The first water to arrive carries whatever settled or entered while the pipe was empty.

6.2 What is already established

  • Meters count air. In a laboratory rig, a water meter registered 0.587 m³ for an air volume of 1.518 m³, and the authors estimate that about 40% of the air in an intermittent system could be billed [6]. A later study measured meters registering 33 to 63% of the air volume as water [7].
  • Empty pipes take in contamination. Sampling in Hubli-Dharwad, India, found elevated turbidity and indicator bacteria when supply was first turned on, and frequent contamination at pressures below 10 psi that the authors attribute to intrusion or backflow [8].
  • In the review, intermittent supply is studied for its health effects (IN009, IN038). Water leak detection methods assume continuous supply.

6.3 What is missing

The published work is on laboratory rigs and city networks. Nothing we found tells a building how much of today's metered volume was water that reached the tank, how much was lost on the way, and how much was air. That question can be answered inside the building with a balance.

Unaccounted volume = volume counted by the meter − rise in tank volume − use during the window

An illustrative case: during one supply window the meter counts 1,800 litres and the tank rises by 1,350 litres with no taps in use. 450 litres is unaccounted for. It either leaked between the meter and the tank or was air. The two can be told apart by timing, because air passes in the first minutes of the window and a leak continues throughout it. Two further signals come from the pump: a pump that runs longer for the same rise in level points to a leak on its delivery side, and a pump drawing low power is running without water.

How much air reaches a given building depends on the network upstream of it, which is why it has to be measured at the building itself.

What an alert would say. "Today's supply window: the meter counted 1,800 litres and the tank received 1,350 litres. 450 litres did not reach the tank." (illustrative)

7 Water gap two: seepage inside walls, read through the wiring

In simple words

A pipe leaking inside a wall cannot be seen. A wet wall, however, lets a very small amount of electricity escape from the wires that run through it. Measuring that escaping electricity can reveal the hidden water leak, long before a damp patch appears.

7.1 What actually happens

  1. In most Indian buildings, water pipes are buried in walls and floor slabs. A weeping joint wets the masonry around it slowly, and nothing shows on the surface for months.
  2. Electrical conduits run through the same walls. Dry plaster and brick are poor conductors. Damp plaster is a much better one.
  3. Where insulation is aged, cracked or jointed inside a damp section, a small current finds a path from the live conductor through the wet wall to earth.
  4. That current returns outside the circuit, so slightly more current flows out on the phase wire than comes back on the neutral. The difference is the leakage.
  5. The leakage is far too small to notice on a bill, and often too small to trip protection where protection is fitted. It grows as the wall gets wetter.

7.2 Why this matters for both utilities

The difference between phase and neutral current is what a residual current device reads in order to trip. Logged continuously at fine resolution, the same quantity serves two purposes. It is an electrical safety signal, and it may be the earliest available sign of a water leak that cannot be seen. One measurement would then report on waste in both utilities.

Humidity also raises leakage, so a wet season could look like a leak. The two differ in what they follow. Leakage caused by a pipe should rise and fall with the water schedule: supply windows, pump runs and tap use. Leakage caused by weather should follow rainfall and humidity. Only a system that records both electricity and water can make that comparison, which is an argument for studying the two together.

7.3 What makes this new

  • In the review: none of the 261 studies relates water leakage to electrical leakage.
  • Nearest existing work: hand-held moisture meters, and an older patent that embeds dedicated sensing wires in walls during construction. Both need access or new wiring. Using the wiring a building already has as a passive indicator of hidden moisture is not described in either.
  • How it is demonstrated: a bench panel of plaster with a cable and a controlled drip shows leakage rising as the wall wets and falling as it dries. The measurement uses a residual current sensor that resolves milliamps.

What an alert would say. "Earth leakage on the second-floor east circuit rises each time the pump runs, in dry weather as well. Seepage is likely in that wall." (illustrative)

8 One measurement architecture for all four

The four gaps look unrelated, but they need a small common set of measurements and one shared idea: the system must know the state of supply before it interprets anything else. A load of 30 kW means one thing on an ordinary afternoon and another thing ten minutes after power returns. A flow of 20 litres a minute means one thing mid-window and another in the first minute of it.

8.1 One day, followed through the system

  1. 06:00, water arrives. The meter starts counting. Supply state for water changes to "just returned". Volume counted in the first minutes with no rise in the tank is logged as air.
  2. 06:00 to 07:30, the tank fills. The meter count is compared with the tank's rise. The difference is the day's unaccounted water. Earth leakage on each circuit is watched for a rise that follows the filling.
  3. 10:00, offices open. Heaters or air conditioners start. Voltage at the board and at the load end gives the wiring loss and path resistance of each monitored circuit under full current.
  4. 13:00, power is cut. Voltage at the board drops to zero and supply state for electricity changes to "off". The forecast keeps running, so the energy not used is known.
  5. 15:00, power returns. Supply state changes to "just returned". Load above the forecast is logged as payback until it settles. The payback ratio for this cut is stored.
  6. End of day. Each estimate is priced by the tariff rules and sent as one plain-language line with one action. The manager confirms or dismisses it, and that answer is kept as a label.

9 How these gaps appear in Jammu and Kashmir

  • Supply is interrupted often. Load-shedding of 8 to 10 hours a day is reported (IN008). Each cut is an instance of the debt described in Section 5, and a building may see several in a day.
  • Losses are already high. Aggregate technical and commercial losses in FY25 were 27.45% for the Jammu utility and 33.54% for the Kashmir utility, against 15.04% nationally. Energy lost inside buildings adds to this and is not counted in it.
  • Two opposite seasons. Jammu's summer load is cooling and Kashmir's winter load is resistive heating. Heating circuits carry high current for long hours through wiring often installed for lighter loads, which is the condition in which joints degrade.
  • Water is intermittent and lossy. About 40% of distributed water was reported lost in 2018. Buildings depend on short supply windows, overhead tanks and pumps.
  • Metering is changing. Prepaid smart meters were ordered for all government offices in December 2025. Interval data from the main meter is becoming available where it was not before.
  • No building-level evidence exists. We found no published measurement of any of the four quantities for a building in the region.

11 Possible extensions

The same search produced further gaps that passed some of the tests. They are listed as optional additions.

  • Billing terms that energy detectors ignore. Power factor is named in 12 studies and logged by 6 prototypes, and none turns it into a cost signal. Maximum demand is named in 3 and detected in none.
  • Voltage as a confounder. Named in 15 studies and corrected for in none. Resistive loads draw less power when voltage sags, which can hide or imitate an anomaly.
  • Regional calendars. Closures and festival breaks are treated as noise to remove. They are also when equipment left on is easiest to see.
  • Waste absorbed into the baseline. Models that retrain on recent data learn lasting waste as normal. No study measures how quickly.
  • Shared meters. Where several departments sit behind one meter, no study asks how little sub-metering is needed to say whose waste it is.
  • Purifiers on low-mineral water. Reverse-osmosis units reject several litres for each litre delivered and are often fitted where the incoming water does not need them.

12 Conclusion

The waste most studied in buildings is the waste that is easiest to see. Going through what 261 studies say they left out points to four kinds that are not seen: heat in wiring, the energy repaid after a power cut, losses around a pipe that is empty most of the day, and seepage inside walls. Each one costs money without appearing on any breakdown, each leads to something more serious than the cost, and each is unmeasured at building level in the work we reviewed.

They share a cause. Methods were built for supply that never stops, and they are being applied where supply stops daily. A detector that first establishes the state of supply, and then reads a few ordinary signals in that light, could measure all four. Section 10 sets out how each can be measured, first on open data and bench rigs and then in a real building in Jammu and Kashmir.
