1. What changed since Round 1

Our Round 1 deck promised ECOGUARD for every building and every kind of waste. The jury found it too broad, and our mentor gave us a clear direction in the 14 November meeting:

  • Pick one environment. We chose one academic block: five classrooms, a computer lab and two offices. Everyone on the jury has sat in these rooms.
  • Cover every connected waste inside it. Electricity and water together, including the pump that links them, and the waste nobody can see.
  • Simulate it and show before vs after. One full year, same events, with and without ECOGUARD, in kWh, litres and rupees.
  • Train on data. We generated a labelled year from public numbers, trained on it, and tested on a second year the model had never seen.
  • Point to the exact problem. Every alert names the room, the cause, the cost and the next step.

The core idea from Round 1 stays the same: learn what normal looks like, flag what is not, explain it in plain words and act on it. What changed is focus and proof.

4. The simulator

The simulator (sensors/sim/) moves the block forward in 15-minute steps. Each step it works out:

  • Weather from IMD climate normals for Jammu, with day-to-day noise, so summers are hot and winters need heaters.
  • People from a weekly timetable, with holidays, semester breaks, cancelled classes and people staying late. The timetable is what is planned; the headcount is what really happened.
  • Indoor climate: each room's temperature and humidity follow the weather, the people inside and the AC (a first-order thermal model). An AC set to 17 °C pulls the room down to 17 °C; an open door stops it reaching 24 °C.
  • Room power from the equipment list: lights, fans, ACs that work harder on hot days, PCs, heaters and standby loads.
  • Power cuts in the JPDCL pattern, with voltage sag at peak hours, and inverter batteries that recharge once power returns.
  • Wiring: each circuit has a resistance, so the room sees a lower voltage than the board. A loose joint raises that resistance slowly.
  • Water: use per person per hour, cleaning in the morning, municipal supply windows, the sump, the pump and the roof tank.
  • Faults: eleven kinds, started at random but at realistic rates (lights left on is common; a failing joint is rare). Each fault keeps a record of exactly how much it wasted. That record is the ground truth.
Every step is driven by a seeded random generator. With the same seed you get exactly the same year. That is what makes a fair before-vs-after test possible: the world is identical, only ECOGUARD's presence changes.

The gateway (sensors/gateway.mjs) turns each step into the same JSON message a real ESP32 node would send, posts it to the backend, and applies any command that comes back (switch off, stop pump, close valve, repair ticket).

5. The model: what should this room be using?

For each room we train one regression model that predicts power from:

  • whether the room is in use, and how many people are inside
  • cooling need above 25 °C and above 30 °C, and heating need below 13 °C
  • whether it is daylight
  • the hour of the day when the room is empty (24 small terms, to learn standby and fridge-like loads)

Training uses one simulated year (seed 11) up to 31 March; the rest is held out. We use Huber regression: it behaves like a normal straight-line fit for ordinary readings but stops large spikes (the waste already hiding in the training data) from dragging the line upwards. The spread of normal readings is measured with the median absolute deviation, which is also not fooled by spikes.

After a power cut, rooms on an inverter are allowed about 0.65 kW extra for 8 hours: that is the battery recharging, not waste. Energy checks pause while the grid is off, because the meters read zero.

7. Heat and damp in the walls (our novel check)

Wiring is never perfect. A healthy classroom circuit here has about 0.14 to 0.29 Ω between the board and the room. When a joint loosens, oxidises or a wire is undersized, that number climbs, and every amp that flows through it turns into heat inside the wall: P = I² × R. At 15 A, an extra 0.3 Ω means about 70 W of heat in one spot, day after day. No bill shows it and no meter separates it, and it is how many electrical fires start.

We need only two voltage readings and one current reading, which the room node already has. ECOGUARD learns each circuit's normal resistance from its first readings and then watches the trend. In the test year it caught all 8 failing joints, typically about 9 days after they started, well before they would heat up enough to fail.

The second hidden check links water and wiring: a pipe leaking inside a wall makes it damp, and a damp wall leaks current to earth. Night water flow plus rising earth leakage on the same floor tells the plumber to look inside the wall, not just at the taps.

8. Acting on what it finds

  • Automatic mode (on by default): after a 15-minute grace period the system acts. Rooms are switched off only when nobody is inside, and only non-essential loads. The pump is stopped on overflow or dry run, and the level sensors then control it so a stuck float switch no longer matters. A floor valve is closed only for a real leak (above 40 L/h), never for a drip.
  • Repair tickets go out at once for hidden waste, leaks and pump faults, with a due time (24 hours by default).
  • People stay in charge. "Not waste" on any alert stops the action and is saved as a label. Automatic mode can be turned off in Settings.
  • Advice is written in English and Hindi from templates. The numbers in it (kW, litres, rupees, CO₂) are always computed in code. If we add a language model later, it only rephrases; it never makes up a number.
  • Forecast: the next 24 hours of use from the timetable and the weather, so the manager sees tomorrow's expected bill and peak hour.

14. Questions we expect, and our answers

Q. How good are the models on real buildings?

On 2017, a year they never saw, the electricity baseline has a median hourly error of 20.8% (CV(RMSE)) and 57.1% of buildings pass ASHRAE Guideline 14, the standard energy auditors use to accept a baseline. Labs pass most often (73.6%); classrooms are hardest because their use follows a timetable the data set does not include. Real 2017 meters also contain real faults, which count as model error here, so the true accuracy is a little better.

Q. What did ECOGUARD find in the real buildings?

In the median building, 13.8% of the electricity is avoidable, and one building in four is above 24.9%. Classrooms waste the most (18.8%). The median building with a cooling meter spends 43.0% of its cooling while closed, and 47 buildings heat and cool at the same time for more than 100 hours a year.

Q. Is this real data?

Both. The models were trained and tested on real meters: 659 real classrooms, labs and offices from the open Building Data Genome 2 data set, two full years of hourly electricity, cooling, heating and water readings (5.5 million training rows). Our own block in Jammu is simulated, because no public data set has room-level occupancy, wiring voltage, earth leakage and water flow together, with every fault labelled. The simulator's numbers come from public sources (IMD, CEA, JPDCL, BEE). The detector does not know which one it is talking to: it receives the same JSON a real ESP32 node would send.

Q. Why simulate at all? Why not use a public dataset?

Public datasets (BDG2, CU-BEMS and others) have whole-building or floor meters, but none of them has occupancy, wiring voltage drop, earth leakage and water flow for the same rooms at the same time, and none of them marks which hours were waste. Without that ground truth you cannot say how many faults you caught. The simulator gives us a full year with every fault labelled, so we can measure recall and precision honestly. Our mentor suggested exactly this.

Q. What is the AI part?

Each room has a learned model of what it should be using for the number of people inside, the outside temperature and the time of day. Anything above that expected use, with nobody to justify it, is waste. On top of that come physics checks the model cannot learn on its own (wiring resistance, earth leakage, minimum night flow, pump state). The advice text is written from templates filled with numbers computed in code, so it can never invent a figure.

Q. Why Huber regression and not a neural network?

Two reasons. It is explainable: we can say exactly how many kW one extra person or one degree of heat adds. And it is robust: the training year already contains waste, and Huber loss stops those spikes from pulling the 'normal' line upwards. On held-out data its error was lower than ordinary least squares in all 8 rooms (Computer Lab: 0.317 kW vs 0.525 kW). We do use deep learning where it pays off: the LSTM day-ahead forecaster, trained across hundreds of real buildings, where there is enough data and nobody needs to read the model's coefficients.

Q. How do you know a room is empty?

From the room node's people count. In the real build that comes from a PIR sensor or the existing CCTV with a person-detection model, and the timetable is a fallback. The simulator gives both a planned headcount (timetable) and the actual one, because classes get cancelled and people stay late.

Q. What if the system switches off a room with people in it?

It cannot. The switch-off command is only sent when the people count is zero, after a 15-minute grace period, and only for non-essential loads. Anyone pressing 'Not waste' stops the action and teaches the model. In the full-year test there were 0 cases of a room being switched off with people inside.

Q. How does 'heat in the walls' work?

Every wire and joint has resistance. Current through resistance makes heat (I² × R) that no meter bills separately. We measure the voltage at the distribution board and at the room, and the current. The difference divided by the current is the resistance of the wiring in between. When a joint loosens or corrodes, that resistance climbs slowly over days. We learn each circuit's normal value and warn when it is 60% higher. The energy is small; the real value is catching a fire risk before it becomes one.

Q. And earth leakage?

A leakage sensor (the same principle as an RCCB) measures current escaping to earth. A healthy circuit leaks almost nothing. When water from a hidden pipe leak soaks a wall, leakage on that floor rises. If at the same time water keeps flowing on that floor at night, we say the leak is probably inside the wall. That combination is our second novel check.

Q. Why is the pump the centre of the water side?

Because it is where energy and water meet. If the float switch sticks, the pump keeps lifting water that pours off the roof: water wasted and electricity wasted to lift it. If the sump is empty, the pump runs dry, burns electricity and damages itself. Our level sensors let ECOGUARD stop the pump in both cases.

Q. How did you get the before-vs-after numbers?

We ran the same year twice: same people, same weather, same power cuts and the same 520 faults. The first run had nobody watching. In the second, every reading went through the real backend and every command came back to the building. Electricity fell 19.2%, water 22.6%.

Q. Why not 100% savings?

Some waste happens before any detector can be sure (a room has to be empty for 30 minutes before we call it waste), some needs a plumber or electrician who takes time, and some is small enough that shutting a whole floor's valve would be worse than the drip. We chose to be right and safe over being fast.

Q. Which waste is hardest to catch?

Lights and fans left on in a classroom for a short gap between classes: the room is empty for less than 30 minutes, so we let it go on purpose (91% caught overall). Before we added the wall temperature sensor, an AC set too cold was the hardest (32% caught from power alone); with the sensor it is 94%. We keep thresholds high on purpose, because a false alert in an occupied room is the fastest way to make staff ignore the system.

Q. What does it cost to deploy?

Per room: one ESP32 board, a current sensor, a voltage sensor at each end of the circuit, a leakage sensor and a relay module, roughly ₹2,500 to ₹3,500. For water: a flow meter per floor and level sensors for tank and sump. The software runs on one small server or a Raspberry Pi on campus.

Q. Can it work without internet?

Yes. The backend runs on the campus network. Readings are small JSON messages every 15 minutes, and the room nodes keep working on their own if the server is down.

Q. What happens during a power cut?

Grid meters read zero, so energy rules pause. Water rules continue. After power returns we watch for loads coming back on in empty rooms (switches left on), and we do not blame inverter rooms for the battery recharge, which is the cost of the outage and not someone's mistake.

Q. How is this different from a smart meter app?

A smart meter tells you how much you used. ECOGUARD tells you which room wasted it, why, what it cost, and fixes it, and it sees waste a meter never shows: heat in wiring, leaks inside walls, and the pump wasting both resources at once.

Q. How does the system get better over time?

Every alert has 'Correct' and 'Not waste' buttons. Each tap is stored as a label and can be downloaded as a CSV. Retraining on those labels tunes the model to this building.
